If you have been hurt in an accident, it is natural to wonder whether you have a viable personal injury claim. The legal system can feel overwhelming, especially when you are focused on recovering. This self-evaluation tool is designed to help you think through the key factors that attorneys consider when evaluating a case.

## What Makes a Personal Injury Case Viable?

Personal injury law is built on a concept called negligence. To have a viable claim, four elements generally need to be present:

1. **Duty**: The other party owed you a duty of care (all drivers owe other road users a duty; property owners owe a duty to lawful visitors; manufacturers owe consumers a duty to sell safe products)
2. **Breach**: They failed to meet that duty (ran a red light, failed to clean up a spill, sold a defective product)
3. **Causation**: That failure caused your injury — not a pre-existing condition or some unrelated event
4. **Damages**: You suffered actual harm — physical injury, medical bills, lost income, pain and suffering

The stronger each of these elements, the stronger your case. The wizard below walks you through questions tied directly to these four pillars.

## Special Considerations by Case Type

**Car accidents** are the most common type of personal injury case. Evidence like police reports, traffic camera footage, and eyewitness accounts can establish fault quickly. Delays in seeking medical care are a common defense tactic — see a doctor promptly.

**Premises liability** (slip-and-fall, negligent security) requires proving the property owner knew or should have known about the dangerous condition and failed to correct it. Incident reports and surveillance footage are critical and may be destroyed if you do not act quickly.

**Medical malpractice** cases are among the most complex. They require expert medical testimony to establish the standard of care and how it was breached. These cases have specialized statutes of limitations (often shorter than standard PI deadlines) and require attorneys with specific experience.

**Product liability** cases hold manufacturers, distributors, or retailers responsible for defective or dangerous products. These cases often involve large defendants with significant resources — experienced counsel is essential.

**Workplace injuries** may be handled through workers' compensation rather than a personal injury lawsuit, depending on your state and the circumstances. However, if a third party (not your employer) was at fault, you may have both a workers' comp claim and a personal injury claim simultaneously.

## What to Do After the Evaluation

Regardless of your result, there are steps that will protect your claim:

**Seek medical attention immediately.** Medical records are the backbone of any injury claim. Gaps in treatment give insurers ammunition to minimize your damages.

**Document everything.** Photograph the scene, your injuries, and any property damage. Keep all medical bills, prescription receipts, and records of missed work. Note down your symptoms and how they affect your daily life.

**Do not give recorded statements to the other party's insurer.** Insurance adjusters are trained to minimize payouts. Anything you say can be used to reduce your claim. Consult an attorney first.

**Preserve evidence.** Surveillance footage is often overwritten within days. Demand that businesses preserve it in writing. In product liability cases, keep the defective product.

**Know your deadlines.** Statutes of limitations are strict. Miss the deadline and your claim is barred — even if liability is crystal clear. Government entity claims often require a notice of claim within 30 to 90 days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What if I feel fine right now but might develop symptoms later?**

Many injuries — especially soft-tissue injuries, concussions, and internal trauma — do not produce obvious symptoms immediately. This is another reason to see a doctor promptly after any accident. A medical evaluation creates a record that links your injury to the event, even if full symptoms develop later.

**Can I handle my own personal injury claim?**

For minor injuries with small medical bills, a self-represented claim against an insurer is possible. But for significant injuries, lost wages, or any dispute about fault, research consistently shows that attorney-represented plaintiffs recover more even after fees. Most PI attorneys offer free consultations — use one.

**What if I cannot afford an attorney?**

Personal injury attorneys work on contingency — they get paid only if you recover. There is no upfront cost. This model exists precisely so that injured people with limited means can access skilled legal representation. You have nothing to lose by consulting with one.
