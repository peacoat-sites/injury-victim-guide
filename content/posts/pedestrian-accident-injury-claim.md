## Why Pedestrian Claims Are Different From Other Car Accident Cases

I want to get this out of the way early because it changes how you should handle everything that follows.

When two cars collide, both drivers typically have insurance, and adjusters are used to trading information between carriers. When a car hits a pedestrian, the injured person usually has no vehicle insurance of their own directly tied to the incident. That shifts the entire dynamic. You're dealing with the at-fault driver's liability coverage, and your interests are directly opposed to that insurer's financial goals. They are not your friend. They are not a neutral party. The adjuster who calls you within 24 hours sounds helpful because being friendly early is a documented tactic to get recorded statements and quick settlements before you understand the full scope of your injuries.

There's also a physical severity issue. A car bumper traveling at 25 mph transfers enormous force to an unprotected human body. Fractures, traumatic brain injuries, internal bleeding, and spinal injuries are common in pedestrian accidents. The problem is that some of these injuries, particularly soft tissue damage and concussions, don't fully manifest for days or even weeks. Accepting a settlement before you know your full diagnosis is one of the most expensive mistakes a pedestrian victim can make.

Finally, comparative fault is a real threat in pedestrian cases. Insurance adjusters are trained to find reasons why you share some responsibility: you were wearing dark clothing, you weren't at a marked crossing, you were looking at your phone. In many states, your compensation can be reduced by your percentage of fault, and in a handful of states, any fault on your part can eliminate recovery entirely. Knowing this dynamic exists is the first step in protecting yourself against it.

I've seen solid cases damaged, sometimes permanently, by what happened in the first three days. Here's what to do immediately.

**Get medical care, even if you feel okay.** Adrenaline masks pain. A soft tissue neck injury or a slow intracranial bleed can feel like nothing until it's serious. Seeing a doctor creates a medical record that connects your injuries to the accident. If there's a gap between the accident and your first medical visit, insurers will argue your injuries happened elsewhere or weren't serious.

**Call the police and get the report number.** Even for lower-speed accidents, a police report documents the scene, the involved driver, and ideally whether citations were issued. Ask the officer for the report number before you leave.

**Document everything at the scene.** If you can safely do so: photograph the intersection, the crosswalk markings, any skid marks, the vehicle, and your injuries. Get contact information from witnesses. Witnesses scatter fast, and their accounts are gold if fault is disputed later.

**Don't give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance.** This is the big one. You are not legally required to speak with the adverse insurer. They will ask to record the call. They will ask questions designed to get you to minimize your injuries or admit some fault. Politely decline and consult with a personal injury attorney before you say anything on record.

**Write everything down.** A simple daily injury journal, noting your pain level, what you couldn't do because of your injuries, medical appointments, and emotional distress, creates a contemporaneous record that's genuinely persuasive later. ## Understanding What Your Claim Is Actually Worth

This is where most people get lost, and honestly, it's where the insurance company wants you to stay confused.

A pedestrian injury claim can include several distinct categories of compensation, often called "damages" in legal terminology.

**Economic damages** are the calculable financial losses: medical bills (current and future), lost wages while you're recovering, and any out-of-pocket costs directly caused by the accident, like transportation to medical appointments or home care assistance.

**Non-economic damages** cover pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment of life, and in severe cases, disfigurement. These don't come with receipts, which is why insurers often undervalue them. But they're real, compensable, and often the largest component of a pedestrian injury settlement.

**Punitive damages** are rare and require egregious conduct, like a driver who was drunk or street racing. They're meant to punish rather than compensate.

I won't throw out dollar ranges because your specific situation matters enormously: the severity of your injuries, your state's laws, the at-fault driver's coverage limits, and whether you have your own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage (more on that below). What I will tell you is that Nolo's personal injury resources offer solid explanations of how damages are calculated if you want to understand the mechanics before talking to an attorney.

## Insurance Coverage: Whose Policy Pays, and What Happens When It's Not Enough

Here's something that surprises a lot of pedestrians: your own auto insurance policy might be relevant even though you weren't in a car.

**The at-fault driver's liability coverage** is the primary source. This is the insurer that will deny, delay, and try to minimize. Their coverage limits cap what you can collect from them, regardless of what your damages actually total.

**Your own uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage** can be a lifeline if the at-fault driver has no insurance or policy limits that don't come close to covering your injuries. Many auto policies extend UM/UIM coverage to policyholders even when they're struck as pedestrians. Check your own policy or ask your insurer directly. This isn't a conflict of interest situation the same way adverse insurance is, but you still want to be careful about what you say and document carefully.

**Medical payments (MedPay) or Personal Injury Protection (PIP)** coverage from your own auto policy can cover medical bills quickly, regardless of fault. PIP is required in no-fault states. Even in fault states, MedPay can fill gaps while the liability claim is being resolved.

**Health insurance** will likely cover your medical treatment, but if you later settle with the at-fault driver's insurer, your health insurer may have a subrogation right, meaning they can claim reimbursement from your settlement. An attorney can help you understand how this affects your net recovery.

## How the Claims Process Actually Unfolds

Step by step, so you know what's coming.

1. **Preserve evidence.** Photographs, witness contacts, the police report, your medical records and bills, pay stubs if you missed work.
2. **Open a claim with the at-fault driver's insurer.** You report the accident, but you don't accept fault or give detailed statements without representation.
3. **Complete your medical treatment or reach maximum medical improvement (MMI).** MMI is the point where your doctors say your condition has stabilized. Settling before MMI is usually a mistake because you don't yet know your total medical costs or long-term prognosis.
4. **Calculate your damages.** All medical bills, projected future treatment costs, documented lost wages, and a reasonable figure for pain and suffering.
5. **Send a demand letter.** This formal letter outlines what happened, who was at fault, your injuries, your damages, and the amount you're demanding to settle. The insurer responds with a counteroffer.
6. **Negotiate.** This is where having an attorney earns its fee. Personal injury attorneys typically work on contingency, meaning you pay nothing unless you win, and their fee comes from the settlement. An experienced attorney often extracts significantly more than an unrepresented claimant, even after the fee.
7. **Settlement or lawsuit.** Most pedestrian accident claims settle without filing a lawsuit. If the insurer won't offer fair value, an attorney can file suit, which changes the insurer's calculus.

One practical tool: keeping your medical records organized throughout this process makes a real difference. Medical records organizers or claim workbooks (the site may earn a commission) help you track every appointment, diagnosis, and bill in one place rather than scrambling through a pile of papers when you need them.
