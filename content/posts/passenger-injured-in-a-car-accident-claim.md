If you were a passenger in a car that got into an accident, you're in a stronger legal position than most people realize. You almost certainly didn't cause the crash. That means you can typically file a [claim against](/hit-by-a-robotaxi-how-to-file-an-injury-claim-against-an-av-company/) the at-fault driver's liability insurance, and in many situations, you have multiple potential sources of compensation, not just one.

That flexibility matters, because passengers often get squeezed out of the claims process. The drivers are arguing over fault, the [insurance companies](/how-insurance-companies-calculate-settlements/) are focused on protecting their own policyholders, and nobody is automatically looking out for you. Understanding where you stand changes that.

## Who Pays When a Passenger Gets Hurt

The short answer: it depends on who caused the crash, what coverage exists, and which state you're in. But let's make it concrete.

If the driver of the car you were riding in was at fault, their [bodily injury](/difference-between-bodily-injury-and-personal-injury-claim/) liability insurance covers your injuries. If the other driver caused the crash, their liability insurance is your primary target. If both drivers share fault, both policies may be on the hook. And if the at-fault driver was uninsured or underinsured, your own auto insurance policy (if you have one) may step in through uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, often abbreviated UM/UIM.

There's also first-party medical coverage to consider. Personal Injury Protection (PIP) and Medical Payments coverage (MedPay) are both available on some auto policies, and either one can pay your medical bills regardless of who caused the accident. PIP is mandatory in no-fault states; MedPay is optional in most others. The vehicle you were riding in may carry either one, and so might your own policy. Check both.

**Example:** Assume you're a passenger and the car you're in is rear-ended by another driver. The other driver carries $50,000 in bodily injury liability coverage. Your injuries require $35,000 in medical treatment plus lost wages of $10,000. You'd file a third-party claim against the other driver's liability policy. That policy would likely cover the full $45,000 in documented damages, assuming liability is clear. If your damages exceeded the $50,000 limit, you'd then look to any underinsured motorist coverage on the vehicle you were riding in, or on your own policy.

## When the Driver Who Hit You Is Someone You Know

This is where things get uncomfortable. If you were a passenger in a friend's or family member's car and they caused the crash, your claim goes against their liability insurance, not against them personally. That's what liability insurance is for. Most people hesitate to pursue this because they don't want to hurt someone they care about. But you're not suing your friend. You're making a claim on a policy they pay premiums on specifically for this situation.

A common misconception: that filing a claim will automatically spike your friend's premiums or ruin their insurance record. The truth is more nuanced. Rate increases depend on the insurer, the state, prior history, and claim specifics. That's genuinely worth a conversation with the driver or their insurer. But it's not a reason to absorb serious medical costs yourself.

## Steps to Take After the Accident

There's an order that matters here, and skipping steps early can hurt you later.

1. **Get medical attention immediately.** Even if you feel okay. Insurance adjusters look hard at any gap between the accident and your first medical visit. A three-day delay gives them an argument that your injuries didn't come from the crash.

2. **Collect information at the scene.** Names, insurance policy numbers, and photos of all vehicles, the road conditions, and any visible injuries. Get the police report number.

3. **Report to your own insurance company.** Even if you're the passenger and you weren't driving anything. Your policy may carry UM/UIM or MedPay that applies. Failing to report promptly can jeopardize that coverage.

4. **Document everything from day one.** A simple notebook or even a notes app works. Date every entry. Record symptoms, doctor visits, missed work days, things you can't do because of your injuries. This record becomes evidence. A structured injury documentation journal (available on Amazon, disclosure: this site may earn a commission) can help if you want something more organized.

5. **Be careful what you say to the at-fault driver's insurance company.** You're not obligated to give a recorded statement to an insurer that isn't your own. They represent the person who may owe you money. Nolo's personal injury resources explain this clearly and are worth reading before you pick up that phone.

6. **Understand your state's statute of limitations.** Most states give you two to three years from the date of the accident to file a lawsuit. Missing that deadline kills your claim, period. Look it up for your specific state now, not six months from now.

## What Your Claim Can Actually Cover

Injury claims for passengers aren't limited to medical bills. Here's what's typically recoverable under a liability claim:

| Type of Damage | What It Covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Medical expenses | Hospital, surgery, rehab, prescriptions | Past and future bills both |
| Lost income | Wages missed while recovering | Self-employed? Document revenue carefully |
| Loss of earning capacity | Future income if injury is permanent | Requires expert testimony in most cases |
| Pain and suffering | Physical pain, emotional distress | No fixed formula; varies widely by case |
| Property damage | Personal items damaged in crash | Separate from injury claim, usually faster |
| Out-of-pocket costs | Transport to appointments, home care | Keep every receipt |

The Insurance Information Institute notes that bodily injury liability claims tend to settle for a range that reflects both economic damages (the bills and lost wages you can document) and non-economic damages (pain and suffering). There's no universal multiplier that reliably predicts a settlement amount, regardless of what you may read online.

## If Multiple Drivers Are at Fault

This scenario is more common than people expect. Say both drivers were partially negligent, one ran a red light and the other was speeding. As a passenger, you can typically pursue both drivers' liability policies. You don't have to pick one.

What complicates this: each insurer will try to point fault at the other driver to reduce their own exposure. You'll be caught in the middle. This is one of the cleaner arguments for getting a personal injury attorney involved, particularly because most work on contingency (meaning they get paid a percentage of your recovery, only if you win). No upfront cost to you.
