Most people hire a [personal injury lawyer](/personal-injury-lawyer-contingency-fee-explained/) exactly once in their lives. They have no baseline for what "normal" looks like, which makes it genuinely hard to spot when something is off. So here's the short answer: if a lawyer pressures you to sign quickly, can't tell you clearly who will actually handle your case, or guarantees you a specific dollar outcome, those are serious problems, and you should walk away.

The rest of this article goes deeper, because the warning signs aren't always dramatic. Some of the worst red flags look almost reasonable until you understand what's really happening.

## The Guarantee Problem

No reputable [personal injury attorney](/personal-injury-attorney-free-consultation-what-to-expect/) will promise you a specific settlement amount. Full stop. Insurance companies have adjusters, defense lawyers, and actuarial data on their side. Outcomes depend on medical records, liability disputes, jurisdiction, comparative fault rules, and a dozen other variables that don't get resolved in a free consultation.

When a lawyer says something like "I'll get you at least $50,000" in a first meeting, they're telling you one of two things: they don't actually understand how [personal injury cases](/how-long-do-personal-injury-cases-take/) work, or they're saying whatever closes the deal. Neither is acceptable when your financial recovery is on the line.

The American Bar Association's guidance on attorney advertising is clear that lawyers cannot make false or misleading statements about likely outcomes. Promising results is a disciplinary violation in most states. If you hear a guarantee, the right move is to thank them for their time and leave.

## Pressure to Sign Immediately

Legitimate attorneys give you time to review a fee agreement. They expect you to ask questions. Some will even encourage you to get a second opinion before signing.

A lawyer who creates urgency -- "the statute of limitations is about to run," "this offer won't last," "I need a signed agreement today" -- should put you on alert. Statutes of limitations for personal injury cases are typically one to three years depending on the state and the type of claim. That's not usually a reason to sign a retainer in a hospital bed on day two. If the deadline is genuinely imminent, a reputable attorney will explain exactly when it is, in writing, so you can verify it yourself.

The pressure tactic works because injury victims are often in pain, stressed, and unfamiliar with the process. That's precisely why some attorneys use it.

## Who Actually Handles Your Case?

This is the question most people forget to ask, and it matters enormously.

Some personal injury firms are built around a recognizable name or heavy advertising budget. The named partner does the TV commercials. Your case gets assigned to a first-year associate or, in some operations, a non-attorney case manager, and you won't meet the senior attorney until (maybe) a few days before trial, if it gets that far.

That's not automatically a catastrophe. Large firms can have excellent associates. But you have a right to know, upfront, who will be your primary point of contact, what their experience level is, and whether the attorney you met with will actually appear on your behalf if the case goes to court. Ask directly. If you get a vague answer, or if the person you're talking to seems annoyed by the question, that's informative.

## Ambulance Chasing and Unsolicited Contact

If a lawyer (or someone claiming to represent one) contacts you after your accident without you reaching out first, that's called solicitation. In most states, direct unsolicited in-person or real-time contact after an accident is prohibited under state bar rules. The person calling may not even be an attorney, they might be a "runner" or "capper," a third party paid to bring clients to a firm, which is illegal in every U.S. jurisdiction.

This matters beyond just the ethics of it. Firms that use runners tend to also do high-volume, low-attention work. They're often looking for quick settlements rather than the best possible outcome for you.

If you've already been contacted this way and signed something, that agreement may be challengeable. Contact your state bar's ethics hotline for guidance.

## Fee Red Flags: What "Normal" Actually Looks Like

Standard contingency fees in personal injury cases are typically 33% of the gross recovery if the case settles before filing a lawsuit. They often rise to 40% if the case goes to litigation, and sometimes higher if it goes to trial or appeal. These percentages vary by state, case type, and firm.

| Stage of Case | Typical Contingency Fee Range |
|---|---|
| Pre-litigation settlement | 25% – 33% |
| Post-filing / litigation | 33% – 40% |
| Trial | 40% – 45% |
| Appeal | 45% – 50% |

These are general ranges. Your state may cap fees by rule (California, for instance, has statutory caps for certain case types). Nolo's personal injury resources have state-by-state breakdowns worth checking before you sign anything.

What should concern you: a fee of 40% at the pre-litigation stage with no explanation, fees calculated on the gross recovery rather than the net (meaning expenses come out of your share, not the attorney's), or a contract that buries case costs in language you can't parse. Ask the attorney to walk through a hypothetical calculation with real numbers before you sign.

**Example:** Assume a $90,000 settlement with $10,000 in litigation costs. Under a "net" fee structure at 33%, the attorney takes 33% of $80,000 (the amount after expenses) = $26,400. Under a "gross" fee structure at 33%, the attorney takes 33% of $90,000 = $29,700, and you then separately cover the $10,000 in costs. That difference is $13,300 out of your pocket. Same percentage, very different result.

## Communication Patterns That Tell You a Lot

A lawyer who doesn't return calls during the intake process, when they're trying to win your business, is showing you exactly how they'll behave once you've signed. That's not pessimism, it's a reasonable inference.

Watch for: calls that go unreturned for more than two business days, staff who can't tell you the status of your case, and situations where you're consistently talking to someone different every time. Personal injury cases can take months or years. You need a team that keeps you informed without you having to chase them.

Also watch for a lawyer who seems eager to settle fast regardless of whether you've reached maximum medical improvement. Settling before your treatment is complete means you might be accepting a number that doesn't account for future medical costs. A good attorney will usually advise waiting.

One last thing worth saying plainly: a bad attorney can do more damage to your case than no attorney. The good news is that most personal injury lawyers are paid only if you win, which aligns their incentives with yours in theory. But incentive alignment isn't the same as competence or integrity. Slow down, ask the hard questions, and don't let anyone rush you into signing on a bad day.
