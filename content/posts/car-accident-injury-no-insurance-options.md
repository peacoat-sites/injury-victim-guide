You get rear-ended at a red light. The other driver gets out, looks at the damage, and then says three words that make your stomach drop: "I don't have insurance." You're hurt, your car is damaged, and suddenly everything you thought you knew about how this works goes out the window. It's a nightmare scenario, but it happens more often than most people realize. According to the Insurance Research Council, roughly 1 in 8 drivers on American roads is uninsured. In some states, that number climbs closer to 1 in 4.

Here's the thing nobody tells you upfront: you're not necessarily out of options just because the other driver has no coverage. You may have more paths to compensation than you think.

## Understanding Your Immediate Position
As of June 2026, 
Before you panic, take a breath. The at-fault driver being uninsured doesn't erase their legal liability. They still caused the accident. They still owe you for your injuries and property damage. The hard part is that collecting from someone with no insurance works very differently than making a standard third-party claim.

You're dealing with an "uninsured motorist" situation now, and your options depend on what's in your own policy, your state's laws, how badly you're hurt, and whether the at-fault driver has anything worth suing for. That last point matters more than people think.

I've seen clients walk away from these situations with full compensation. I've also seen people leave serious money on the table because they didn't know what to ask for. Let's go through every realistic option.

Most people don't read their auto policy until something goes wrong. But right now, open that document or call your agent and ask specifically about two coverages.

**[Uninsured Motorist Coverage (UM)](/uninsured-motorist-claim-process/):** This is coverage you may have bought to protect yourself exactly for this. UM pays for your bodily injuries when the at-fault driver has no insurance. Many states require insurers to offer it, and some require you to carry it. If you have it, this is your fastest, cleanest path to compensation for medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering. You're filing with your own insurer, but as an injured party, not the at-fault party.

**Underinsured Motorist Coverage (UIM):** This kicks in when the at-fault driver has some insurance, just not enough. Uninsured situations fall under UM, not UIM, but the distinction matters.

**MedPay or Personal Injury Protection (PIP):** These pay your medical bills no matter who caused the accident. PIP is required in no-fault states like Florida, Michigan, and New York. MedPay is optional in many others. Neither requires you to prove fault, which makes them valuable when fault gets disputed or the claims process drags on.

Don't know what you carry? Call your insurer right now and ask them to walk you through your declarations page.

Here's something worth knowing: even on a UM claim with your own insurer, you're negotiating against them. Your insurer's adjusters get paid to keep payouts low, even on UM claims. Understanding [how insurance companies calculate settlements](/how-insurance-companies-calculate-settlements/) before you engage can genuinely shift the outcome.

## Suing the At-Fault Driver Directly

You can absolutely sue an uninsured driver in civil court. The harder question is whether you'll actually collect if you win.

A judgment against someone with no insurance, no savings, no assets, and no property is called an "uncollectible judgment." You win the lawsuit, you get a piece of paper saying they owe you money, and then you spend more time and money chasing it down. That's a real scenario you need to weigh.

But don't assume the uninsured driver is broke. Some people drive uninsured because they're disorganized or let their policy lapse during a rough month financially. They might own a home, have a bank account, or earn a steady income. If a judgment lands against them, you can often garnish wages or place a lien on property in many states.

Before going this route, talk to a personal injury attorney. Most offer free consultations and work on contingency, meaning they don't get paid unless you do. An attorney can run an asset check on the at-fault driver and give you a realistic read on whether a lawsuit makes sense. The [American Bar Association's guidance](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_education/) on finding legal help is a solid starting point.

## Health Insurance and Other Coverage Sources

If you have health insurance, use it now. Don't wait for the liability situation to sort itself out before getting treatment. Delaying care hurts you medically and kills any future claim because it creates gaps in your medical records.

Your health insurer may have subrogation rights, meaning they can seek reimbursement from any settlement you receive later. That's a problem for later, and it's usually negotiable. Right now, getting treated matters most.

Other sources worth checking:

- **Employer disability coverage:** If injuries keep you out of work, short-term disability through your employer may cover lost wages while your claim sits pending.
- **Hospital financial assistance programs:** Many hospitals have charity care programs that reduce or eliminate medical bills. Ask billing directly.
- **Medicare or Medicaid:** If you qualify, these can cover treatment costs short-term.

## What to Do in the Days Right After the Accident

Actions you take in the first 72 hours can seriously affect your options down the road.

1. **Call the police at the scene.** Get a report filed. This creates an official record and documents that the other driver was uninsured.

2. **Get medical attention immediately.** Even if you feel okay, see a doctor. Soft tissue injuries and concussions often don't show their full symptoms for days.

3. **Document everything at the scene.** Photograph the vehicles, the road, traffic signals, weather, the other driver's license and registration (insurance card or not), and any visible injuries on yourself.

4. **Collect witness information.** Names and phone numbers from anyone who saw it happen.

5. **Notify your own insurer the same day.** Report the accident and specifically say the other driver was uninsured. Ask your adjuster to walk you through your UM and MedPay coverages.

6. **Keep a daily injury journal.** Write down symptoms, pain levels, limitations, how the injury affects your daily life. Insurance adjusters and juries respond to detailed, consistent documentation. [How to document injuries after an accident](/how-to-document-injuries-after-accident/) covers this process in depth.

7. **Don't give a recorded statement to the other driver's attorney or anyone else without consulting an attorney first.** Even UM claims can turn adversarial.

8. **Consult a personal injury attorney.** Especially important if injuries are serious. Most evaluate cases for free.

## How Injury Severity Affects Your Strategy

Not all accidents are equal, and how badly you're hurt should shape how aggressively you pursue compensation.

For minor injuries with low treatment costs and full recovery in a few weeks, filing a UM claim with your own insurer might be all you need. The process is relatively straightforward, and the amounts may not justify hiring an attorney.

For moderate to serious injuries, broken bones, spinal trauma, head injury, surgery, long-term rehab, every option deserves serious consideration. These are cases where understanding [factors that increase settlement value](/factors-that-increase-settlement-value/) really matters. Permanent impairment, lost earning capacity, and ongoing medical needs can shift what fair compensation looks like.

Nolo's research shows injuries resulting in permanent disability or disfigurement carry substantially higher potential compensation than soft tissue injuries. That's precisely why documentation and legal guidance matter so much in serious cases.

For context on where your situation might land, [average personal injury settlement amounts](/average-personal-injury-settlement-amounts/) gives useful perspective, though every case is genuinely different.
