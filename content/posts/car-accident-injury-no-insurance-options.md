You get rear-ended at a red light. The other driver gets out, looks at the damage, and then says three words that make your stomach drop: "I don't have insurance." You're hurt, your car is damaged, and suddenly everything you thought you knew about how this works goes out the window. It's a nightmare scenario, but it happens more often than most people realize. According to the Insurance Research Council, roughly 1 in 8 drivers on American roads is uninsured. In some states, that number climbs closer to 1 in 4.

Here's the thing nobody tells you upfront: you're not necessarily out of options just because the other driver has no coverage. You may have more paths to compensation than you think.

## Understanding Your Immediate Position

Before you panic, take a breath and assess what you're working with. The at-fault driver being uninsured doesn't erase their legal liability. They still caused the accident. They still owe you for your injuries and property damage. The hard part is that collecting from someone with no insurance is a very different process than making a standard third-party claim.

You're now dealing with what the insurance industry calls an "uninsured motorist" situation, and the options available to you depend on a few key factors: what coverage you carry on your own policy, the laws in your state, the severity of your injuries, and whether the at-fault driver has any personal assets worth pursuing.

I've seen clients walk away from these situations with full compensation. I've also seen people leave money on the table because they didn't know what to ask for. Let's walk through every realistic option.

Most people don't read their own auto policy closely until something goes wrong. That's understandable. But right now, you need to open that policy document or call your agent and ask specifically about two coverages.

**Uninsured Motorist Coverage (UM):** This is coverage you may have purchased to protect yourself exactly for this situation. UM coverage pays for your bodily injuries when the at-fault driver has no insurance. Many states require insurers to offer it, and some states require you to carry it. If you have it, this is your fastest, cleanest path to compensation for medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering. You're filing a claim with your own insurer, but you're doing so as an injured party, not the at-fault party.

**Underinsured Motorist Coverage (UIM):** This is slightly different. It kicks in when the at-fault driver has some insurance, just not enough to cover your damages. Uninsured situations fall under UM, not UIM, but it's worth understanding the distinction.

**MedPay or Personal Injury Protection (PIP):** These coverages pay your medical bills regardless of who caused the accident. PIP is required in no-fault states like Florida, Michigan, and New York. MedPay is an optional add-on in many other states. Neither requires you to prove fault, which makes them valuable when fault is disputed or the claims process is dragging on.

If you don't know what coverages you carry, call your insurer right now and ask them to walk you through your declarations page. Don't wait.

One thing I want to flag: even when you're filing a UM claim with your own insurer, you're still negotiating against them. Your insurer has adjusters whose job is to pay as little as possible, even on UM claims. Understanding [how insurance companies calculate settlements](/how-insurance-companies-calculate-settlements/) before you engage in that process can genuinely change the outcome for you.

## Suing the At-Fault Driver Directly

You can absolutely sue an uninsured driver in civil court. The harder question is whether you can actually collect anything if you win.

A judgment against someone with no insurance, no savings, no significant assets, and no property is called an "uncollectible judgment." You win the lawsuit, you get a piece of paper saying they owe you money, and then you spend more time and money trying to chase it down. That's a real possibility you need to weigh.

That said, don't assume the uninsured driver is broke. Some people drive without insurance because they're disorganized or because they let their policy lapse during a hard month financially. They might own a home, have a bank account, or earn a steady income. If a judgment is entered against them, you may be able to garnish wages or place a lien on their property in many states.

Before pursuing this route, talk to a personal injury attorney. Many offer free consultations and work on contingency, meaning they don't get paid unless you do. An attorney can run an asset check on the at-fault driver and give you a realistic read on whether a lawsuit is worth pursuing. The [American Bar Association's guidance](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_education/) on finding legal help is a solid starting point if you don't know where to begin.

## Health Insurance and Other Coverage Sources

If you have health insurance, use it. Don't wait for the liability situation to sort itself out before getting treatment. Delaying care hurts your health and, frankly, it hurts any future claim you might make because it creates gaps in your medical records.

Your health insurer may have subrogation rights, meaning they can seek reimbursement from any settlement you later receive. But that's a problem for later, and it's often negotiable. Right now, getting treated matters most.

Other sources worth checking:

- **Employer disability coverage:** If your injuries keep you out of work, short-term disability insurance through your employer may cover a portion of your lost wages while your claim is pending.
- **Hospital financial assistance programs:** If you're uninsured or underinsured, many hospitals have charity care programs that can reduce or eliminate medical bills. Ask the billing department directly.
- **Medicare or Medicaid:** If you qualify, these can cover treatment costs in the short term.

## What to Do in the Days Right After the Accident

The actions you take in the first 72 hours can seriously affect your options down the road. Here's a straightforward sequence to follow:

1. **Call the police at the scene.** Get a police report filed. This creates an official record of the accident and documents that the other driver was uninsured.

2. **Get medical attention immediately.** Even if you feel okay, see a doctor. Some injuries, especially soft tissue injuries and concussions, don't show their full symptoms for days.

3. **Document everything at the scene.** Photograph the vehicles, the road, traffic signals, weather conditions, the other driver's license and registration (even if there's no insurance card), and any visible injuries on yourself.

4. **Collect witness information.** Names and phone numbers from anyone who saw the accident happen.

5. **Notify your own insurer the same day.** Report the accident and specifically mention the other driver was uninsured. Ask your adjuster to walk you through your UM and MedPay coverages.

6. **Keep a daily injury journal.** Write down your symptoms, pain levels, limitations, and how the injury is affecting your daily life. Insurance adjusters and juries both respond to detailed, consistent documentation. [How to document injuries after an accident](/how-to-document-injuries-after-accident/) covers this process in depth.

7. **Don't give a recorded statement to the other driver's attorney or to anyone beyond your own insurer without consulting an attorney first.** Even UM claims can become adversarial.

8. **Consult a personal injury attorney.** This is especially important if your injuries are serious. Most will evaluate your case for free.

## How Injury Severity Affects Your Strategy

Not all accidents are equal, and the severity of your injury should shape how aggressively you pursue compensation.

For minor injuries where treatment costs are low and you fully recover within a few weeks, filing a UM claim with your own insurer may be all you need. The process is relatively straightforward, and the amounts at stake may not justify hiring an attorney.

For moderate to serious injuries, including broken bones, spinal injuries, head trauma, or injuries requiring surgery or long-term rehabilitation, every option on the table deserves serious consideration. These are the cases where understanding [factors that increase settlement value](/factors-that-increase-settlement-value/) really matters, because permanent impairment, lost earning capacity, and ongoing medical needs can significantly change what fair compensation looks like.

Nolo's personal injury resources note that injuries resulting in permanent disability or disfigurement carry substantially higher potential compensation than soft tissue injuries, which is precisely why documentation and legal guidance matter so much in serious cases.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I still get compensation if both me and the at-fault driver are uninsured?

Yes, but your options narrow considerably. You can still sue the at-fault driver directly and potentially pursue a judgment against their assets. You won't be able to use your own UM coverage if you didn't carry it. In no-fault states, PIP coverage on your own policy would also be unavailable. Being uninsured yourself can also affect how courts view your claim in some states. This is a situation where speaking with an attorney quickly is especially important.

### Will filing a UM claim raise my insurance rates?

It depends on your state and your insurer. Many states prohibit insurers from raising rates solely because you filed a UM claim, since you were the victim, not the at-fault party. However, policies vary. Ask your agent directly and get the answer in writing before filing if you're concerned. The protection UM coverage offers typically outweighs the risk of a modest rate increase.

### What if the uninsured driver left the scene (hit and run)?

A hit-and-run is treated similarly to an uninsured motorist situation for claims purposes. Your UM coverage should apply, though most policies require you to have made physical contact with the other vehicle and to have reported the accident to police promptly. Read your specific policy language carefully. Some states have additional protections for hit-and-run victims through state guarantee funds.

### How long do I have to file a claim or lawsuit?

Every state has a statute of limitations for personal injury claims, typically between one and three years from the date of the accident. Missing this deadline almost certainly bars you from recovering anything. Don't assume you have more time than you do. UM claims with your own insurer also have policy-specific reporting deadlines that can be much shorter. Act promptly.

### What if my injuries are still developing and I don't know the full extent of the damage yet?

Don't settle anything before you've reached what doctors call "maximum medical improvement," the point where your condition has stabilized and your care team can give a realistic long-term prognosis. Settling too early locks in a number that may not account for future surgeries, ongoing therapy, or permanent limitations. This is another reason why having an attorney review your case before you sign anything is genuinely valuable.
