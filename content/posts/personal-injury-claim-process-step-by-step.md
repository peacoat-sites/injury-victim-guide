You wake up in a hospital bed after a car accident that wasn't your fault. A nurse hands you paperwork. Your phone has fourteen missed calls. Somewhere across town, an insurance adjuster is already opening a new claim file with your name on it. That adjuster's job, and I say this having done it for twelve years, is to close your file for as little money as possible. Your job is to understand what comes next before you make a single move that could hurt you.

The personal injury claim process isn't complicated, but it is unforgiving if you stumble at the wrong moment. Let's walk through it together.

---

## What Happens Immediately After the Injury (The First 72 Hours Matter Most)

The decisions you make in the first three days after an injury can shape your entire claim. I've seen strong cases fall apart because of what someone did, or didn't do, right after the accident.

**Get medical attention. Today.** Not tomorrow, not "when you feel worse." Gaps in treatment are one of the first things an insurance company will point to when they want to reduce your settlement. If you saw a doctor on the day of the accident and then waited two weeks to follow up, expect the adjuster to argue that your injuries couldn't be that serious.

At the scene, if you're physically able, document everything. Take photos of vehicle positions, property damage, visible injuries, road conditions, and any skid marks. Get the names and phone numbers of witnesses before they disappear. If police respond, get the report number, not just a business card.

Notify your own insurance company about the accident, even if the other driver was at fault. Most policies require prompt notification as a condition of coverage. This doesn't mean you have to give a detailed recorded statement immediately. It means you alert them that an incident occurred.

One practical tool I recommend to clients is keeping a daily injury journal from day one. Write down your pain levels, what activities you couldn't do, and how the injury is affecting your sleep, work, and relationships. Courts and insurance companies both respond to specific, documented details. A general statement like "my back hurt" is far less compelling than "I was unable to lift my 3-year-old daughter for six weeks." You don't technically need a lawyer for every personal injury claim. A minor fender-bender with a small medical bill and a cooperative insurance company might be something you can handle yourself. But for anything involving significant injuries, disputed fault, multiple parties, or insurance company pushback, having an attorney in your corner changes the dynamic entirely.

Most personal injury attorneys work on a contingency fee basis. This means you pay nothing upfront. The attorney takes a percentage of your final settlement or court award, typically somewhere between 25% and 40% depending on the complexity of the case and whether it goes to trial. If you recover nothing, you owe nothing in attorney fees. This arrangement makes legal representation accessible to people who couldn't otherwise afford it.

When you consult with attorneys, and most offer free initial consultations, ask specific questions. How many cases like mine have you handled? Will you personally work on my case or will it be handed to a junior associate? How do you communicate with clients, and how often? These aren't rude questions. They're reasonable. An attorney who gets defensive is telling you something.

The American Bar Association's guidance on finding qualified legal help emphasizes checking an attorney's state bar membership status and any disciplinary history before you commit. That's five minutes of research that could save you a lot of grief.

## Building Your Claim: Evidence, Medical Records, and the Damages Calculation

This is the phase most people don't understand well, and it's where the real value gets built or lost.

Your attorney, or you, if you're handling the claim yourself, will gather several categories of evidence. Police reports establish the official account of what happened. Medical records connect your injuries directly to the incident. Bills and invoices document your economic losses. Photographs, witness statements, and expert opinions fill in the rest.

**Economic damages** are the straightforward ones. Medical expenses, lost wages, cost of future treatment, costs of hiring help for things you can no longer do yourself. These are calculated from actual records and bills.

**Non-economic damages** are trickier. Pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment of life, loss of consortium (the impact on your relationship with a spouse). These don't come with receipts. Attorneys typically use one of two methods to calculate them: the multiplier method (multiplying your economic damages by a number, often between 1.5 and 5, based on injury severity) or the per diem method (assigning a daily dollar value to your pain and multiplying by the number of days you've suffered). Neither is a guarantee of what you'll receive. They're starting points for negotiation.

Don't settle before you've reached what doctors call "maximum medical improvement," or MMI. MMI means your condition has stabilized and your doctor can give a realistic picture of long-term effects. Settling before MMI means you might be leaving future medical costs on the table, and once you sign a release, that claim is closed forever.

## The Demand Letter and Negotiation Process

Once your medical treatment is complete, or at least stabilized, your attorney will send the insurance company a demand letter. This document lays out the facts of the incident, your injuries, your medical treatment and costs, your lost wages, and your non-economic damages. It closes with a demand for a specific dollar amount to settle the claim.

The insurance company will almost always respond with a counteroffer that is significantly lower than your demand. This is expected. It's not an insult. It's the opening of a negotiation.

Back-and-forth negotiations can take weeks or months. The adjuster on the other side is trained to look for reasons to reduce the value: pre-existing conditions, gaps in treatment, inconsistencies in your medical records, anything that creates doubt about the connection between the accident and your injuries. Your attorney counters these arguments with evidence.

In my experience, the majority of personal injury claims settle without going to court. Nolo's personal injury resources put the figure at roughly 95% of cases, which tracks with what I observed during my time on the other side of the desk. Insurers generally prefer certainty over the risk and expense of trial. But that statistic shouldn't make you complacent. A fair settlement requires leverage, and leverage comes from having strong evidence and a credible attorney willing to litigate if necessary.

## When Negotiation Fails: Filing a Lawsuit and What Comes After

If negotiations break down, the next step is filing a lawsuit. This doesn't mean you're going to trial. It means the formal legal process has begun, which often creates new pressure on the insurance company to settle.

After filing, the case enters a phase called **discovery**. Both sides exchange information and evidence. You may be deposed, meaning you'll answer questions under oath with both attorneys present. Medical experts may be hired by each side. This phase can take many months.

Most cases settle during or after discovery, once both sides have a clearer picture of how a trial would likely go. If no settlement is reached, the case goes to trial. A judge or jury hears the evidence and decides both liability (who was at fault and by how much) and damages (what you're owed).

One concept worth understanding is **comparative negligence**. In most states, if you were partially at fault for the accident, your damages are reduced by your percentage of fault. In a state with pure comparative negligence, you could be 80% at fault and still recover 20% of your damages. In a modified comparative negligence state, you're typically barred from recovery if you're 50% or 51% or more at fault (the threshold varies by state). This is one reason why what you say at the scene, to police and to insurers, matters so much.

Throughout all of this, one deadline governs everything: the **statute of limitations**. This is the legal deadline for filing your lawsuit. Miss it, and your claim is almost certainly gone forever. The deadline varies by state and by type of claim, but for most personal injury cases it's between one and three years from the date of injury. Don't assume you have time. Talk to an attorney early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a personal injury claim typically take?

There's no honest single answer to this because it depends on too many variables. A straightforward car accident claim with clear liability and limited injuries might settle in three to six months. A complex case involving serious injuries, disputed fault, or multiple defendants can take two to four years, especially if it goes to trial. Don't let anyone guarantee you a timeline.

### Should I give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company?

You're generally not legally required to give a recorded statement to the other party's insurance company. Their adjuster will ask for one, and they're trained to do it quickly, before you've had time to think. Recorded statements can be used to minimize your claim. If you have an attorney, this communication should go through them. If you don't yet have an attorney, it's worth consulting with one before agreeing to any recorded statement.

### What if I can't afford a doctor?

You can't build a personal injury claim without medical documentation, full stop. If you don't have insurance and can't pay out of pocket, look into a few options. Some medical providers will treat patients under a "letter of protection," meaning they agree to wait for payment until your claim settles. Medicaid may cover your treatment if you qualify. Community health centers provide care on a sliding-scale fee basis. Your attorney, once you have one, may be able to help coordinate this.

### What does it mean to sign a release?

A release is the document that formally ends your claim in exchange for the settlement payment. Once you sign it, you cannot go back and ask for more money, even if your injuries turn out to be worse than expected. This is why settling before you've reached maximum medical improvement is so risky. Read any release carefully and make sure you understand every word before you sign.

### Can I handle my personal injury claim without an attorney?

Yes, and for minor claims it may make sense. If your injuries were minor, treatment is complete, and the insurance company is offering what seems fair, you may not need representation. But be realistic about what you don't know. Insurance adjusters handle hundreds of claims a year. Most injured people handle one in a lifetime. For any claim involving significant injury, surgery, disability, lost wages, or disputed liability, having professional representation typically results in a better outcome, even after deducting attorney fees.

The personal injury claim process rewards the people who are organized, patient, and informed. It punishes people who act out of fear or urgency and accept the first offer just to make it go away. You don't have to be a legal expert to protect yourself. You just have to know enough to ask the right questions, document everything, and get qualified help before making decisions you can't undo. That's what this process, at its core, is really about.
