Three months. That's what the adjuster told Maria when she called to check on her rear-end collision claim. Three months turned into eleven. By the time her case settled, she'd missed two car payments, borrowed money from her mother, and had a collection notice from her physical therapist sitting on the kitchen table.

I spent twelve years on the other side of that phone call. I know exactly why adjusters say "three months" even when they're looking at a file that screams fourteen. And I want to save you from the version of this story where you make financial decisions based on a timeline someone gave you to keep you calm.

Let's talk about what actually drives settlement timelines. Not the optimistic version, not the scary version, just the real one.

## The Range Is Wider Than Anyone Tells You

Straightforward car accident claims with clear liability, soft tissue injuries, and cooperative insurance companies can settle in 60 to 90 days. I've seen it happen. But "straightforward" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and most cases don't qualify.

On the other end: cases involving serious injuries, disputed fault, multiple defendants, or litigation run two to four years. A 2022 analysis from the Insurance Research Council found the median time to close represented injury claims was climbing, not shrinking, as insurers got more aggressive about documentation requirements and causation challenges.

Most people land in the messy middle. Six months to eighteen months is genuinely common for moderate-injury claims involving back-and-forth negotiation but no trial. That's a wide window. Frustrating to hear? Yeah. But I'd rather give you that honest range than the "usually just a few months" line that sets people up for Maria's situation.

## What's Actually Eating the Clock

This is where I want to spend real time, because understanding these factors gives you some actual ability to influence them.

**The single biggest delay: your medical treatment isn't finished.**

Most experienced personal injury attorneys won't file a demand package until you've reached "maximum medical improvement," or MMI. That's the point where your doctor says your condition has stabilized, whether you've fully recovered or not. Here's why: if you settle before you know the full extent of your injuries, you might be signing away rights to compensation for surgeries or treatments you don't even know you'll need yet.

This one thing alone can add months. Break your wrist in January, still in occupational therapy in August, and your attorney isn't dragging their feet. They're waiting for your medical picture to get clear. It's the right call, even when it's agonizing.

**The insurance company's pace.**

Once your attorney sends a demand letter, the insurance company has 30 days to respond under most state laws (some states give them longer). Here's what most people don't realize: that response is rarely a settlement offer. It's usually a counteroffer, often a low one, designed to test whether you'll blink. Multiple rounds of negotiation are normal. I've seen cases where the back-and-forth alone took four months.

**Liability disputes.**

If the other side contests who was at fault, or argues that you were partially responsible, that adds time and complexity. Comparative negligence arguments (meaning the other side says you were, say, 30% at fault) have to be factored into every number. These don't always require trial, but they extend negotiation significantly.

**Multiple parties.**

If your accident involved more than one defendant, or if multiple insurance policies are in play (your own underinsured motorist coverage, the at-fault driver's policy, a commercial vehicle policy), coordinating between them is genuinely complicated. I've worked files where just identifying all the applicable coverage took six weeks.

**The litigation decision.**

If settlement negotiations break down and your attorney files a lawsuit, you're on the court's timeline now. Civil court dockets in most jurisdictions are backed up. Twelve to twenty-four months from filing to trial is not unusual. Discovery alone (the formal exchange of evidence and depositions) can run six to nine months. That said, most cases that go into litigation still settle before trial. Sometimes literally in the courthouse hallway.

## The Things You Control (More Than You Think)

A lot of this is outside your hands. But not all of it.

Getting medical treatment promptly matters more than people realize. Gaps in treatment are one of the insurance industry's favorite arguments. If you waited three weeks after your accident to see a doctor, you'll hear about it. Not from your doctor, from the adjuster, who will argue the delay proves you weren't really hurt. See a doctor quickly, follow through on recommended treatment, and don't stop going before your doctor clears you.

Document everything. Relentlessly. Keep a daily pain journal. Photograph your injuries, your car damage, anything relevant. Save every text and email related to the accident. Respond to your attorney's requests fast. One thing I watched slow cases down repeatedly was the client. Signed authorizations, missing medical records releases, unreturned calls. Every week you take to return something your attorney needs is a week added to your timeline. I'm not blaming anyone, I know people are dealing with injuries and financial stress and regular life. But if you want to move your case forward, be the easiest client your attorney has ever had.

## If You're Waiting on a Settlement Check Right Now

After you sign a settlement agreement, there's still a waiting period before the check arrives. Your attorney's office has to receive the check, process it, satisfy any medical liens (money owed back to health insurers or Medicare/Medicaid), pay attorney fees, and then cut you a check for the remainder. That process can take two to six weeks after signing. Sometimes longer if there are lien disputes.

Lien resolution is something a lot of people don't anticipate. If your health insurance paid for treatment related to your injury, they often have a right to be reimbursed from your settlement. A good attorney will fight to reduce them, which takes time but directly increases your net recovery. Don't rush your attorney through this part to get your check faster. It's usually worth it.

## When to Ask Questions

The American Bar Association's guidance on working with attorneys is clear that clients have a right to regular updates on their cases. If your attorney isn't communicating and you genuinely don't know the status of your claim, that's a legitimate concern worth raising directly. Most delays are explainable. Some aren't.

If nothing has happened on your file in 60 days and nobody has told you why, it's reasonable to call and ask for a status update in writing. Not because you're being difficult, because staying informed is your right and it helps you make good financial decisions in the meantime.

Most clients who feel frustrated by slow timelines are frustrated because they weren't prepared for the reality of how long this takes. The CDC's injury data shows that millions of Americans are going through some version of this process every year, and the financial stress during the waiting period is real and documented. You're not being impatient. The system is genuinely slow.
