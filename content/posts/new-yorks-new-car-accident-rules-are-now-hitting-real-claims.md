If you filed a [car accident](/car-accident-injury-no-insurance-options/) lawsuit in New York after May 26, 2026, you're playing by a completely different set of rules than anyone who filed the day before. Governor Hochul signed Part EE of Chapter 58 that day, and the law took effect immediately for new cases. The first contested claims are now working through the courts, and November 27, 2026 marks a deadline for insurer rate-approval requirements tied directly to the reform's promise of lower premiums. That combination makes right now a critical moment for anyone hurt in a New York car crash to understand exactly what changed and what it means for their claim.

The short version: two of the biggest tools injury victims used to recover compensation have been either eliminated or heavily restricted. If you had a soft-tissue injury that healed, you may no longer have a viable pain-and-[suffering claim](/car-accident-pain-and-suffering-claim/) at all. If you shared any blame for the crash, the question of *how much* blame now carries stakes it never did before. These aren't minor procedural tweaks. They're structural changes to who recovers, how much, and under what conditions.

Kantor Gullo Law, writing in November 2026, described the reform as a dramatic departure from decades of New York injury law, noting that the changes affect not just what you can claim but whether you can claim anything at all for non-economic losses. That framing is accurate.

## The 90/180-Day Threshold Is Gone. Here's What That Actually Means.

New York's "serious injury" threshold was always the gatekeeper to pain-and-suffering claims. To sue for non-economic damages after a car accident, you had to prove your injury met one of several defined categories, things like permanent loss of a body part, significant disfigurement, or a fracture.

The 90/180-day category was the most accessible one for everyday crash victims. It covered people who couldn't do their normal daily activities for 90 of the 180 days following the accident. That's not a small group. Whiplash patients, people with herniated discs that eventually resolved, anyone who missed months of work but eventually recovered. Under the old system, those people could potentially clear the threshold and pursue a pain-and-suffering claim.

That category no longer exists. As Segal McCambridge explained in their August 2026 analysis, the elimination specifically targets temporary impairments rather than permanent ones. If your injury healed, the reform's drafters essentially decided the legal system shouldn't be your avenue for pain-and-suffering recovery. You may still have a property damage claim or a wage loss claim. But the non-economic piece, the compensation for what you went through physically and emotionally, is gone for temporary injuries filed under the new rules.

This is the change that affects the most people. Soft-tissue injuries are the most common result of car accidents. Not every whiplash is a [permanent disability](/permanent-disability-settlement-amounts/).

## The 50% Fault Bar: A Hard Stop on Recovery

Under New York's old pure comparative negligence system, fault was shared on a sliding scale. If you were 60% at fault for a crash, you could still recover 40% of your damages. That system wasn't perfect, but it meant almost everyone who was hurt walked away with something.

New CPLR §1411(b) ends that. If a jury finds you more than 50% responsible for the accident, you get zero in pain-and-suffering damages. Not reduced. Zero.

The practical consequences are significant. Insurance adjusters and defense lawyers will now push hard to build a contributory fault narrative. Speeding even slightly. Not wearing a seatbelt. Making a turn without signaling. These things always mattered, but now they matter in a binary way at the 51% threshold.

There's also a separate, harsher rule for specific situations:

| Claimant Situation | Fault Finding | Pain & Suffering Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Standard claimant | 50% or less | Proportional recovery allowed |
| Standard claimant | More than 50% | $0 |
| Uninsured OR impaired claimant with shared fault | Any shared fault | Capped at $100,000 |

The $100,000 cap on uninsured or impaired claimants is a separate provision entirely. If you were driving without insurance or while impaired and you share any fault for the accident, your pain-and-suffering recovery tops out at $100,000 regardless of how severe your injuries are.

## Why This Law Passed: The Lobbying and the 'Fraudemic' Argument

Understanding the political context helps explain why these rules are structured the way they are. New York's auto insurance premiums have been among the highest in the country for years. Insurers and rideshare companies argued that a culture of staged accidents and inflated soft-tissue claims was driving costs up for everyone.

Rideshare companies including Uber lobbied in New York for exactly these kinds of changes, citing what they called a "fraudemic" of staged accidents. That framing shaped the political debate considerably. The argument was straightforward: too many questionable soft-tissue claims, particularly the 90/180-day variety, were costing the system money, and legitimate policyholders were paying for it through higher premiums.

Whether that justification holds up over time will depend partly on what happens to premiums now. The November 27, 2026 rate-approval requirement is designed to hold insurers accountable for delivering on that promise. Before that date, insurers could raise rates without prior state approval. After it, any rate increase requires sign-off from regulators first. If premiums don't come down meaningfully, the trade-off that injury victims were asked to make will look very different in retrospect.

## What Claimants Filing Right Now Should Understand

The cases working through the courts right now are the first real test of how these rules play out in practice. Juries haven't set patterns. Judges haven't issued definitive rulings on how the new threshold categories will be interpreted. Everything is in flux.

A few things are clear, though. Documentation matters more than ever. If you're injured and the question of fault is even slightly ambiguous, every piece of evidence establishing the other driver's responsibility becomes more valuable. Photos, witness statements, dashcam footage, police reports. The 50% bar creates a hard cutoff where none existed before.

The nature of your injury matters more too. Under the old rules, a soft-tissue injury that resolved over five or six months could still support a serious injury claim through the 90/180-day pathway. Under the new rules, you generally need to show something that qualifies under one of the remaining permanent or significant impairment categories. As Kennedys Law noted in their November 2026 analysis of the legislative changes, the remaining serious injury categories still exist but the removal of the 90/180-day route meaningfully narrows who qualifies.

Timing matters for existing cases too. If your lawsuit was filed before May 26, 2026, the old rules still apply. This distinction is not minor. Two people injured in the same type of accident, one who sued in April and one who sued in June, are operating under completely different legal frameworks.

If you've been hurt in a New York car accident since May 26, 2026, talking to a personal injury attorney who's already handling claims under the new rules isn't just a good idea. It's the only way to get an accurate read on where your specific situation stands. The law is new, the cases are new, and the stakes are real.
