Post-traumatic stress disorder is a legitimate, compensable injury in a [personal injury claim](/how-long-does-a-personal-injury-claim-take-to-settle/), and insurance companies know it. That's the first thing to understand. The second is that PTSD claims are routinely underpaid or denied not because they lack legal merit, but because claimants don't know how to document them. This article is about fixing that.

A serious accident doesn't just break bones or tear ligaments. For many people, the psychological damage outlasts the physical damage by years. Flashbacks, hypervigilance, sleep disruption, panic attacks triggered by driving, the smell of gasoline, the sound of brakes. These aren't character weaknesses. They're recognized clinical injuries, and under tort law in every U.S. state, they're recoverable damages.

## Why Insurers Fight PTSD Claims Hard

Insurance adjusters are trained to view psychological [injury claims](/social-media-addiction-personal-injury-claims-explained/) with skepticism. The internal reasoning goes like this: physical injuries leave visible evidence. X-rays, surgical reports, MRI findings. PTSD doesn't photograph. That gap between "you can't see it" and "therefore it's exaggerated" is where a lot of legitimate claims get squeezed.

The other complication is causation. An adjuster will look for any pre-existing mental health history and argue that the accident didn't cause your PTSD, it just aggravated something that was already there. This is a common and often cynical argument. Under what most states call the "eggshell plaintiff" rule (sometimes called the thin skull rule), a defendant takes the plaintiff as they find them. If you had a prior anxiety disorder and the accident pushed you into full clinical PTSD, the defendant is still liable for that worsening.

Don't assume a pre-existing mental health history kills your claim. It complicates it. There's a difference.

## The Diagnosis Problem (and How to Solve It)

You cannot build a PTSD claim on your own description of your symptoms. You need a formal diagnosis from a licensed mental health professional, ideally a psychiatrist or a psychologist with experience in trauma. The diagnosis should use DSM-5 criteria (the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, published by the American Psychiatric Association). DSM-5 requires documented evidence of exposure to a traumatic event, intrusion symptoms, avoidance, negative changes in cognition or mood, and hyperarousal. All four clusters need to be present.

Here's what the documentation path actually looks like:

1. **See your primary care physician first** if you haven't already, and report your psychological symptoms at every appointment following the accident. This creates a contemporaneous medical record. The word "contemporaneous" matters legally; it means the record was created close in time to the event, before anyone had litigation strategy in mind.

2. **Get a referral to a mental health professional.** A psychiatrist can diagnose and prescribe; a psychologist can diagnose and provide therapy. For claim purposes, you want both the diagnosis and the treatment record.

3. **Be consistent and complete in your symptom reporting.** Don't minimize at appointments. If you're having nightmares four nights a week, say four nights a week. [Medical records](/medical-records-release-sign-or-not/) that understate symptoms will be used against you later.

4. **Keep a symptom journal.** Date it, write in it regularly, and be specific: "Tried to drive to the grocery store, had a panic attack at the intersection where the accident happened, turned around and went home." A notebook like a [daily journal designed for medical tracking](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=medical+symptom+journal) can help you stay organized. 5. **Document functional impairment.** PTSD damages aren't just about how you feel, they're about what you can't do. Lost wages because you can't return to work, the inability to drive (and what that costs you in transportation or lost opportunity), relationships strained or broken, activities you've abandoned.

## What Your Claim Can Actually Include

PTSD damages in a personal injury case fall into two broad buckets: economic and non-economic.

**Economic damages** are the ones with receipts. Therapy costs, psychiatry visits, medication, lost income if PTSD keeps you from working, transportation costs if you can no longer drive. These are added up with documentation and are generally less contested once the diagnosis is established.

**Non-economic damages** cover pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment of life, and loss of consortium (the impact on your marriage or domestic partnership). These don't have receipts. They're calculated by the jury, or negotiated in settlement, using either a multiplier applied to your economic damages or a per-diem method that assigns a dollar value to each day of suffering. Neither method is mandated by law; both are tools.

**Example:** Assume medical specials (all medical bills) of $45,000 and lost wages of $20,000, for a combined economic total of $65,000. A multiplier of 2.5 applied to economic damages produces a pain-and-suffering figure of $162,500, bringing the total claim value to roughly $227,500. The multiplier used (anywhere from 1.5 to 5 or higher in catastrophic cases) depends on severity, duration, treatment consistency, and credibility of the treating providers. This is an illustration of the math, not a prediction of outcome. Actual results vary enormously.

| Damages Category | What It Covers | How It's Documented |
|---|---|---|
| Medical treatment (past) | Therapy, psychiatry, medication | Bills, EOBs, pharmacy records |
| Medical treatment (future) | Projected ongoing care | Life care plan or expert testimony |
| Lost wages | Income lost due to PTSD symptoms | Pay stubs, employer letter, tax returns |
| Loss of earning capacity | Career trajectory impact if permanent | Vocational expert |
| Pain and suffering | Emotional distress, daily impairment | Journals, treating provider testimony |
| Loss of enjoyment of life | Activities abandoned, relationships damaged | Personal testimony, witness statements |
| Loss of consortium | Impact on spouse/partner | Spouse testimony |

## The Independent Medical Examination Trap

If your claim is significant, the defense will likely request an Independent Medical Examination (IME). The name is a misnomer. The examining doctor is chosen and paid by the insurance company, and the purpose of the examination is not to treat you or even to fairly assess you. It's to generate a report that minimizes your injuries. The [American Bar Association's public guidance on personal injury claims](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_education/) flags the IME process as one of the areas where claimants most benefit from legal representation.

You are generally required to attend an IME if your case is in litigation. A few practical points: bring your attorney or have them advise you beforehand, don't offer information beyond what's asked, and understand that everything you say and do (including in the parking lot) may be observed. IME doctors who evaluate psychological claims will frequently conclude that your symptoms are exaggerated, that they predate the accident, or that you've had "maximum medical improvement." Your treating providers' records are the antidote to a one-time IME that claims otherwise.

## Statute of Limitations: Don't Wait

Every state has a deadline for filing a personal injury lawsuit. Miss it, and you generally lose the right to sue, regardless of how strong your claim is. Statutes of limitations for personal injury vary by state, typically running between one and three years from the date of the accident or from the date the injury was discovered.

PTSD sometimes falls into a "discovery" gray area. Some people don't develop full clinical PTSD symptoms until weeks or months post-accident, and diagnosis can lag further still. In those situations, some states allow the clock to start from when the injury was or should have been discovered. Don't count on this exception to save you. Talk to an attorney well before any deadline.

The [CDC's injury data resources](https://www.cdc.gov/injury/wisqars/) don't address legal deadlines, but they provide context for the scale of traumatic injury in the U.S., which is relevant to understanding why these cases exist and why the legal framework around them has developed the way it has.
