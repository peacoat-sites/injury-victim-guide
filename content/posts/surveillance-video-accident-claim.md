Surveillance footage can make or break a [personal injury claim](/difference-between-bodily-injury-and-personal-injury-claim/). If a camera captured your accident, that video may be the single most valuable piece of evidence you'll ever have access to, but only if you act fast enough to preserve it.

Most businesses overwrite their surveillance recordings on a rolling cycle. Depending on the system, that cycle might be 24 hours, 72 hours, or 30 days. Some high-end systems store longer. Many small retailers store almost nothing at all. The window between your accident and the moment that footage disappears is often shorter than people expect, and there's no way to recover footage that's already been overwritten.

## Why Footage Disappears So Fast

Security systems today are almost all digital, running on DVR or NVR setups (digital video recorder or network video recorder). These systems record continuously and automatically overwrite older footage when storage fills up. A busy gas station might overwrite footage in 48 hours. A large retail chain might keep 30 to 90 days. Municipal traffic cameras vary by city contract. There's no federal standard requiring any private business to keep footage for any set period.

The practical result: if you slip and fall in a [grocery store](/supermarket-slip-and-fall-lawsuit/) on a Monday and don't send a preservation request until the following Friday, there's a real chance the footage is already gone. This isn't necessarily sinister. It's just how the technology works. But it does mean that delay costs you in a way that nothing else in your case will.

One thing most people don't know is that the business's [insurance company](/insurance-company-delay-tactics-injury-claim/) may already be looking for that footage. Adjusters are trained to investigate claims quickly, and securing video evidence is often their first move. They're not doing it as a favor to you.

## The Preservation Demand: Your First Move

A preservation demand (also called a litigation hold letter or spoliation notice) is a written document you send to whoever controls the footage, telling them to stop any automatic overwriting and to preserve all video from a specific date, time, and location. You don't need a lawyer to send one, but having a lawyer send it makes it harder to ignore.

Here's the specific sequence to follow, and where things go wrong at each step:

1. **Identify every potential camera source.** Don't think only about the business where you were hurt. Think about neighboring storefronts, ATMs, parking lot cameras, traffic signals, transit authority cameras, and residential doorbell cameras (Ring, Nest, and similar). The CDC's injury data shows falls and traffic incidents account for a huge share of injury claims, and both types of incidents frequently happen in locations with multiple overlapping camera views.

2. **Send written notice to each owner within 48 to 72 hours if possible.** Address it to the business or property owner, the manager on duty, and if you can find out, their insurer or legal department. Send it by certified mail with return receipt AND by email if you have an address. Keep copies of everything.

3. **Be specific in what you request.** Name the date, time range (ask for a buffer, like two hours before and after the incident), and all cameras that could have captured the area. Vague requests get vague responses.

4. **Follow up in writing.** If you don't get a written confirmation that footage has been preserved, send a second notice referencing the first. Create a paper trail.

5. **If you have a lawyer, have them file a spoliation letter immediately.** This letter formally puts the other party on notice that intentional or negligent destruction of evidence may expose them to sanctions in court, including an instruction to the jury that they can assume the missing footage would have supported your case. This is a real legal doctrine, not a bluff, and it changes how businesses behave.

What goes wrong: people call the business instead of writing, get a verbal assurance, and then find out the footage is gone. Phone calls leave no record. Write everything down.

## What the Footage Can Actually Show (and What It Can't)

A clean, well-angled video of your accident can establish: how the incident happened, what conditions existed beforehand, how you responded, whether staff was aware of the hazard, and how you appeared immediately after. That last point matters more than people expect. Post-incident behavior captured on video goes both ways. It can show you were clearly in pain, or it can show you walking out easily and then claiming severe injury later. Adjusters watch for inconsistencies between video and medical records constantly.

What footage often can't show: the exact speed of a vehicle, the precise height of a fall hazard, the chemical composition of a wet floor, or what happened before the camera's field of view. This is why video is evidence, not a verdict. It almost always requires supporting documentation.

**Example:** Assume a shopper falls near the entrance of a store at 2:14 PM. The store has two cameras covering that area. Camera 1 shows the floor was wet and no wet floor sign was visible at 1:50 PM. Camera 2 captures the fall itself, including the claimant landing and lying on the floor. Combined, those two clips establish a hazardous condition, the absence of a warning, and the fall mechanism. That's strong evidence of negligence, though a jury still weighs it alongside medical records, witness statements, and the store's maintenance logs.

## Timelines and Storage: What to Expect by Source

Different camera sources have very different retention windows. Here's a realistic breakdown:

| Camera Source | Typical Retention Period | Who to Contact |
|---|---|---|
| Retail store (small/independent) | 24–72 hours | Store manager, in writing |
| Retail chain (large box store) | 30–90 days | Loss prevention department, certified mail |
| Restaurant or bar | 7–30 days | Owner or manager |
| Municipal traffic camera | 30–72 hours (varies by city) | City traffic or public works department |
| Transit authority (bus, subway) | 30–72 hours typically | Transit authority records office |
| Residential doorbell (Ring, Nest) | 60 days on cloud (Ring Protect plan) or less | Homeowner directly |
| Parking garage | 30–90 days | Garage operator or property management |
| Hotel or casino | Up to 90 days (high-security sites longer) | Security department, written request |
| Rideshare/delivery vehicle dashcam | Varies; often auto-deletes unless driver saves | Direct to driver and company legal team |

The American Bar Association's guidance on pretrial discovery emphasizes that electronic evidence, including surveillance video, is subject to the same preservation duties as physical evidence once litigation is reasonably anticipated. If the other party destroys footage after receiving a preservation demand, their attorney has a problem.

## When the Insurance Company Already Has the Footage

This is the scenario people don't prepare for enough. If you were injured at a commercial property, the property's liability insurer may have dispatched a field adjuster within 24 hours. Field adjusters are trained to do three things immediately: take recorded statements, photograph the scene, and secure surveillance footage. If you haven't yet preserved it, they may have. But they're not sharing it with you unless litigation compels them to.

Don't assume that because the insurer has the footage, you'll eventually see it. They may use it to evaluate your claim internally and never produce it voluntarily. Once you retain an attorney and litigation begins, formal discovery (the process by which both sides exchange evidence) can compel production of any footage they have. But that takes time, and the goal is to get to it before anyone has to sue.

If you're approached early for a recorded statement, understand that you're under no obligation to give one before you've consulted a lawyer. What you say in a recorded statement, combined with what video shows, can be used to challenge your claim. That's not paranoia. That's how the process works.
