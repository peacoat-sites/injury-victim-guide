You got hurt. You're lying on the couch, your car is still at the body shop, and your boss just texted asking when you're coming back. The answer is: you don't know. And the worst part? Every day you're not working is money you're not making. What most injury victims don't realize until it's almost too late is that those lost paychecks aren't just a personal hardship. They're a compensable loss, meaning the person or company responsible for your injury may owe you that money back. Lost wages are one of the most undervalued parts of a personal injury claim, and insurance adjusters know exactly how to lowball them.

## What "Lost Wages" Actually Covers in a Personal Injury Claim

People hear "lost wages" and think it just means the days they called out sick after the accident. It's actually a broader category than that.

Lost wages, in a personal injury context, includes any income you were unable to earn because of your injury. That means:

**Hourly and salaried pay.** The most straightforward category. You missed five days of work. You can document your hourly rate or salary and multiply it out.

**Self-employment income.** Freelancers, contractors, and small business owners can claim lost income too, though proving it takes more documentation. A plumber who can't swing a wrench for six weeks isn't just losing "wages," he's losing contracts, repeat customers, and business revenue.

**Sick and vacation days you were forced to use.** This one surprises people. If you burned through your paid time off to cover your recovery, you may still be able to recover that lost value. You had those days as a benefit you earned. The accident took them from you.

**Bonuses and commissions you missed.** If you were on track for a quarterly sales bonus and your injury knocked you out of contention, that lost opportunity has real value.

**Overtime you would have worked.** This one's harder to prove, but if your employer can document that you regularly worked overtime, that income is part of your loss.

**Future lost earning capacity.** This is the big one. If your injury permanently limits what you can do or how much you can earn, that ongoing loss becomes a major component of your claim. A 35-year-old electrician who suffers a severe spinal injury isn't just out two weeks of pay. He may have lost decades of peak earning years.

If you want to understand why so many injury victims feel shortchanged, read this section carefully. Understanding [how insurance companies calculate settlements](/how-insurance-companies-calculate-settlements/) is the single most powerful thing you can do for your claim.

For simple wage loss, adjusters typically want:

1. A letter from your employer confirming your salary or hourly rate and the days you missed.
2. Recent pay stubs, usually two to four weeks worth.
3. Medical documentation confirming you were actually unable to work during that period.

Sounds reasonable. Here's where it gets tricky.

**They'll fight your doctor's timeline.** If your physician says you needed six weeks off and you took eight, the adjuster will argue that extra two weeks wasn't "medically necessary." They'll use their own medical reviewers to push back on your doctor's judgment.

**They'll discount self-employment income.** Without a traditional employer, you have to prove lost income through tax returns, invoices, contracts, and client statements. Adjusters know this is harder to document and they'll offer pennies on the dollar hoping you take it.

**They'll ignore your lost earning capacity entirely.** This is the most expensive part of many claims, and it requires an expert, often a vocational rehabilitation specialist or an economist, to calculate. Adjusters don't volunteer to hire those experts for you. They'll settle fast before you realize what your long-term losses look like.

**They'll use your gross income, not your net.** Wait, that's actually in your favor. Courts generally use gross (pre-tax) income for calculating wage loss because taxes and withholdings complicate the picture. But some adjusters try to use net figures, especially in informal negotiations.

## Step-by-Step: How to Document Lost Wages the Right Way

Documentation wins claims. I've watched good cases fall apart because the victim couldn't produce the paperwork to back up their loss. Start this process as soon as possible after your accident.

**Step 1: Get a letter from your employer.**
This should confirm your position, your hourly rate or annual salary, your normal work schedule, and the specific dates you missed. Ask HR directly. Most employers are familiar with this request.

**Step 2: Pull your most recent pay stubs.**
Gather at least four to six weeks of recent stubs. They establish your baseline income, including any regular overtime, shift differentials, or bonuses.

**Step 3: Get a written statement from your doctor.**
Your physician needs to provide documentation stating that your injury prevented you from working and for what period. Without this, the insurance company can argue your time off was a personal choice, not a medical necessity.

**Step 4: Keep a daily log.**
Write down each day you were unable to work, what work you would have done, and how the injury specifically prevented it. For self-employed people especially, this log can become critical evidence. **Step 5: Document any business loss separately.**
If you're self-employed, compile invoices you couldn't fulfill, client emails you had to turn down, contracts you lost, and your prior year tax returns showing your average income. A CPA letter can also help establish what your business typically earns.

**Step 6: Don't return to work before you're medically cleared.**
Returning early might seem responsible, but it tells the insurance company your injury wasn't that serious. Follow your doctor's guidance. Your health comes first, and your claim follows directly from your medical record.

## Lost Wages vs. Lost Earning Capacity: Know the Difference

These two concepts sound similar but they're calculated in completely different ways and they serve different purposes in a claim.

**Lost wages** refers to income you've already lost between the date of the accident and the date your case resolves. It's a backward-looking, concrete calculation.

**Lost earning capacity** (sometimes called loss of future earnings) refers to income you will lose going forward because your injury has permanently reduced what you can do or earn. It's a forward-looking, projected calculation.

| | Lost Wages | Lost Earning Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| **Timeframe** | Past losses only | Future losses |
| **Documentation needed** | Pay stubs, employer letter, medical note | Expert testimony, vocational analysis, economic projections |
| **Calculation method** | Rate x days missed | Projected career earnings minus reduced earning potential |
| **Who proves it** | You and your employer | Vocational expert, economist |
| **Severity of injury needed** | Any injury | Usually permanent or long-term |

Lost earning capacity claims are often the largest single component in serious injury cases. The CDC's injury data consistently shows that unintentional injuries are a leading cause of long-term disability in working-age adults, which means for a significant percentage of injury victims, the financial impact extends far beyond a few missed paychecks.

## Common Mistakes That Shrink Your Lost Wages Recovery

In my experience, these are the errors I see most often, and they're all avoidable.

**Waiting to file a claim.** Every state has a statute of limitations, a legal deadline for filing a personal injury claim. Miss it and you lose your right to recovery entirely. You can check [personal injury statute of limitations by state](/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations-by-state/) to find your state's specific deadline.

**Returning to work against medical advice.** Financial pressure is real. I understand it. But working through pain when your doctor has restricted you creates a documented inconsistency that adjusters will exploit.

**Accepting the first offer too quickly.** Early settlement offers rarely account for lost earning capacity. They're designed to close the file before you understand the full scope of your losses. According to Nolo's personal injury resources, victims who consult an attorney before settling typically receive significantly more than those who negotiate alone.

**Not accounting for self-employment income properly.** If you report income differently on your taxes than you actually earn (a common pattern among cash-based freelancers and contractors), documenting your real losses becomes complicated. Work with an accountant and be transparent with your attorney.

**Settling before maximum medical improvement.** "MMI" is the point at which your doctor says your condition is as healed as it's going to get. Settling before that point means you're agreeing to a number before anyone fully knows what your losses are.
