Most people assume that once both sides say "yes" to a number, they're essentially done. They're not. Settlement is an agreement to pay, not the payment itself, and the gap between those two things is where a lot of frustration, confusion, and occasionally real financial harm can happen.

The good news: the post-settlement process is predictable. It moves through a defined sequence of steps, and knowing what comes next means you won't be blindsided when your attorney asks you to sign paperwork before any money changes hands, or when you discover that your health insurer wants a cut of your award. Here's what actually happens after a [personal injury settlement](/lost-wages-in-personal-injury-settlement/) is reached, in the order it typically unfolds.

## You Sign the Release First

Before any check is cut, the defendant's [insurance company](/why-insurance-company-denied-my-injury-claim/) will prepare a settlement release. This is a legal document, usually 3 to 10 pages, in which you agree to give up all future claims against the defendant related to this incident. All future claims. That includes injuries you don't know about yet, complications that emerge later, related property damage, the works.

This is the document that matters most in the entire settlement process, and it deserves your full attention. Common mistakes: signing without reading the scope of the release (some releases try to cover co-defendants or related parties not originally part of the case), agreeing to confidentiality clauses without understanding what you can and cannot say publicly afterward, and overlooking language that could affect unresolved workers' compensation claims.

Your attorney will review the release before you sign, and they should walk you through every material term. If something in that document gives you pause, say so. Negotiating release language is normal.

## What Happens to the Money

Once the signed release is returned to the insurer, the clock starts on payment. The defendant or their insurer typically has 30 days to issue the settlement check, though this varies by state. Some states have statutes requiring payment within a specific window; others don't.

That check doesn't go directly to you. It goes to your attorney's trust account (sometimes called an IOLTA account, which stands for Interest on Lawyers' Trust Accounts). This is required by state bar rules, and it protects everyone. From the trust account, your attorney works through the following:

1. Satisfying any outstanding liens (more on this below)
2. Deducting their [contingency fee](/how-much-does-personal-injury-lawyer-cost/), which the American Bar Association's guidance on fee agreements describes as typically ranging from 25% to 40% depending on case stage and complexity
3. Deducting case costs (filing fees, expert witness fees, medical records, deposition transcripts, etc.)
4. Issuing your disbursement check

You should receive a written settlement statement itemizing every deduction. Demand it if your attorney doesn't provide one automatically. It's your money and you have a right to see the math.

**Example:** Assume a gross settlement of $120,000. The contingency fee is 33% (a common percentage once litigation has started), and case costs are $8,500. Medical liens total $22,000, negotiated down from $30,000.

- Gross settlement: $120,000
- Attorney's fee (33%): $39,600
- Case costs: $8,500
- Medical liens: $22,000
- **Net to client: $49,900**

The numbers change substantially based on liens and costs. Someone walking away from a $120,000 settlement with $50,000 is not unusual. This is not a failure of the legal process, it's the actual math, and you should know it going in.

## Liens: The Most Underexplained Part

A lien is a legal right that a third party holds against your settlement because they paid for something related to your injury. Health insurers, hospitals, Medicare, Medicaid, and workers' compensation carriers can all have lien rights.

Here's what most people don't realize: your attorney is legally required to identify and satisfy valid liens before disbursing your funds. Distributing money around a lien isn't just bad practice, it can expose both you and your attorney to liability.

The most significant liens come from federal programs. Medicare's right to reimbursement is governed by the Medicare Secondary Payer Act, which is federal law. It takes precedence over state law. If Medicare paid for treatment related to your accident, you must resolve that lien or face the possibility of Medicare recovering twice what it paid, plus interest and penalties. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) maintains a process for this through their Medicare Secondary Payer Recovery Portal, and there are formal steps for requesting a final demand and negotiating compromises. It moves slowly, often adding weeks to the timeline.

Private health insurer liens vary by state. Many states have anti-subrogation laws or made-whole doctrines that limit the insurer's right to reimbursement if the settlement didn't fully compensate you. The Insurance Information Institute notes that subrogation rules differ significantly by state, which is exactly why this requires a licensed attorney familiar with your jurisdiction.

The lien negotiation process, more than anything else, is what separates a 6-week settlement disbursement from a 4-month one.

## Typical Timeline

| Stage | Typical Duration | What Can Slow It Down |
|---|---|---|
| Release drafted and reviewed | 1-2 weeks | Complex release language, multiple defendants |
| Release signed and returned to insurer | 1-5 days | Client availability |
| Insurer issues settlement check | 1-4 weeks | State payment deadlines, insurer processing |
| Check clears attorney trust account | 3-7 business days | Standard bank hold |
| Lien resolution | 2-12 weeks | Medicare/Medicaid involvement, hospital disputes |
| Final disbursement to client | 1-3 days after liens resolved | Client contact, address, banking info |
| **Total (straightforward case)** | **4-8 weeks** | |
| **Total (Medicare/Medicaid liens)** | **3-6 months** | |

These are estimates based on common case patterns, not guarantees. Structured settlements (where payments are spread over time rather than paid in a lump sum) follow a completely different timeline and involve annuity contracts, which is a topic on its own.

## Tax Considerations You Can't Ignore

The general rule under IRS guidelines: compensatory damages for physical injury or physical sickness are not taxable. So money you receive for medical bills, pain and suffering from a physical injury, and lost wages tied to a physical injury is typically tax-free.

Exceptions exist. Punitive damages are taxable. Emotional distress damages that are not tied to a physical injury are taxable. Interest that accrues on a settlement is taxable. If you deducted medical expenses in a prior tax year and are now recovering those same medical costs in a settlement, you may owe taxes on that portion (the IRS refers to this as the "tax benefit rule").

Don't take tax guidance from your personal injury attorney unless they're also a tax professional. Consult a CPA or tax attorney about your specific settlement before you file your return.
