Yes, you can sue your landlord for lead poisoning, and in many cases, you have strong legal footing to do it. Lead exposure from rental housing is one of the most serious and well-documented causes of preventable injury in the country, and landlords carry specific legal duties to prevent it. If yours failed those duties and you or your child got sick, that's not just a tragedy. It may be a compensable injury.

The harder questions are what you need to prove, what your claim might be worth, and how long you have to act. Those answers vary by state, by the age of your housing, and by whether your landlord had actual knowledge of the hazard. Let's get into it.

## Why Landlords Have Legal Duties Around Lead

Lead paint in residential housing was banned for consumer use in the United States in 1978. Any building constructed before that year may contain lead-based paint, and the older the building, the more likely it is. This isn't controversial or disputed, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and the EPA have documented this for decades.

Federal law requires landlords renting pre-1978 housing to disclose any known lead-based paint or hazards to tenants before the lease is signed. That obligation comes from the Residential Lead-Based Paint Hazard Reduction Act of 1992 (also called Title X), and the EPA and HUD jointly enforce it. Landlords must give tenants the EPA pamphlet "Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home," disclose what they know, and include specific language in lease agreements. Skipping any of those steps is itself a federal violation, separate from any [personal injury claim](/what-is-a-personal-injury-claim/).

Beyond the federal disclosure rules, most states have their own landlord-tenant statutes that require landlords to maintain rental housing in a habitable condition. Lead hazards, especially deteriorating or peeling paint, typically violate those habitability standards. Some states, like New York and Massachusetts, have even more aggressive lead laws that impose affirmative duties to test and remediate lead paint regardless of whether the landlord actually knew about it. If you're in one of those states, your position is considerably stronger.

## What You Actually Need to Prove

Suing a landlord for lead poisoning is a negligence claim at its core. That means you need to show four things: the landlord owed you a duty, they breached it, that breach caused your injury, and you suffered actual damages. Each element matters.

**Duty and breach** are usually the easiest part. If your building was built before 1978, the federal disclosure duties apply automatically. If the landlord failed to disclose known hazards, didn't give you the EPA pamphlet, or let deteriorating lead paint go unaddressed after you reported it, that's a breach. Documented complaints to the landlord, in writing, through a property manager, or even by text, are worth their weight in gold here.

**Causation** is trickier. You have to connect the lead in your blood to your specific apartment, not just lead exposure in general. This is where environmental testing of your unit and medical records documenting elevated blood lead levels work together. An environmental inspector can test paint, dust, and soil. Your doctor's blood lead level (BLL) results give you the medical evidence. Expert witnesses, typically an environmental health specialist or a toxicologist, tie those two pieces together for a court or insurance adjuster.

**Damages** can be significant in lead cases. Lead causes irreversible neurological harm in young children. The damages categories typically include:

- Medical expenses (past and future, including ongoing monitoring and intervention)
- Developmental and cognitive impairment (quantified through neuropsychological testing)
- Special education costs
- Lost future earning capacity (this is often the largest component in a child's case)
- Pain and suffering

Adults can also suffer lead poisoning, though children under six bear the most severe developmental consequences because their brains are still forming.

## The Evidence You'll Need to Build

Don't wait to start gathering evidence. If you suspect lead poisoning, here's the order of steps that matters:

1. **Get medical testing first.** Your child's pediatrician can order a blood lead level test. The CDC currently identifies a blood lead reference value of 3.5 micrograms per deciliter (µg/dL) as the threshold for concern in children, though no level is considered safe. Get this documented and keep copies of all lab results.

2. **Request environmental testing of your unit.** You can contact your local health department, many will conduct inspections for free or low cost when a child has an elevated BLL. You can also hire a private EPA-certified lead inspector. Keep the report.

3. **Notify your landlord in writing.** Send a letter or email stating the problem, what you've found, and what you're requesting. This creates a record that the landlord had notice. If they ignore it or stall, that inaction can support your claim.

4. **Document everything.** Photographs of peeling or deteriorating paint, your complaints to management, any responses (or non-responses), and your moving history all matter.

5. **Consult a personal injury attorney.** Lead cases are complex. They require expert witnesses, involve medical causation arguments, and landlords are typically represented by insurance companies with experienced adjusters. Nolo's personal injury resources are a good starting point for understanding your rights before you make any calls.

One thing people often skip: keeping a contemporaneous journal of symptoms, medical appointments, and behavioral or developmental changes in an affected child. A simple dated notebook or a [medical records organizer](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=medical+records+organizer) (note: this site may earn a commission on qualifying purchases) can make a material difference when your attorney is building a damages timeline months later.

## What These Cases Are Worth

There's no honest single number here. Lead poisoning settlements and verdicts vary enormously based on the severity of exposure, the child's age and documented developmental harm, the landlord's degree of culpability, and the state where you're filing. What can be said with confidence: cases involving young children with significantly elevated blood lead levels and documented cognitive or developmental harm have historically produced some of the largest residential injury settlements and verdicts in the country.

The table below shows the general damages categories and what drives value up or down in each:

| Damages Category | What Increases Value | What Decreases Value |
|---|---|---|
| Medical expenses | High BLL, ongoing treatment, neurological workup | Minimal documented symptoms |
| Cognitive/developmental harm | Neuropsych testing showing deficits, school records | No testing, mild exposure |
| Future lost earnings | Child plaintiff, significant IQ loss documented | Adult plaintiff, short exposure |
| Special education costs | Documented IEP, learning disabilities | No school-based evidence |
| Pain and suffering | Severe symptoms, duration of exposure | Low BLL, brief tenancy |
| Punitive damages | Landlord knew and concealed hazard | Landlord had no knowledge |

If your landlord violated the federal disclosure requirements, you may also be able to recover up to three times your actual damages under Title X. That's not guaranteed, but it's on the table when there's a clear statutory violation.

## Statute of Limitations: Don't Sleep on This

Most personal injury claims have a statute of limitations of two to three years. For lead poisoning, the clock question gets complicated because the harm is often not immediately obvious. Children may not be tested for years after the exposure, and developmental delays can take time to manifest.

Most states apply a "discovery rule" in latent-injury cases like this. The clock starts when you knew or reasonably should have known about the injury and its possible cause, not necessarily when the exposure happened. That can extend your window, but it doesn't eliminate it, and courts can and do disagree on exactly when knowledge was "imputed."

Check your state's specific deadline. The [personal injury statute of limitations by state](/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations-by-state/) page has this broken down clearly. If you're anywhere near the edge of your window, talk to an attorney before you do anything else.

## Who Has a Harder Case

Not every lead exposure situation supports a lawsuit, and you deserve a straight answer on this.

Your case gets harder if: the building was built after 1978 (lead paint is less likely, though not impossible from other sources like plumbing fixtures or soil); you can't connect the elevated blood lead level to your specific rental property (maybe you lived in multiple units or the exposure source is disputed); there's no documented complaint history showing the landlord had notice of a hazard; or your blood lead levels, while elevated, are low enough that proving measurable harm becomes difficult.

Your case also gets harder if significant time has passed and you no longer have access to the unit for testing. Courts won't speculate about what the paint conditions were years ago without evidence.
