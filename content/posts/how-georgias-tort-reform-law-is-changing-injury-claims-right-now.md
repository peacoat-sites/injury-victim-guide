That's the reality for injury victims filing claims in Georgia right now.

Governor Brian Kemp signed SB 68 and SB 69 on April 21, 2025, describing the package as a necessary correction to a litigation environment that was driving up insurance costs and producing "nuclear verdicts" topping $10 million. By January 1, 2026, the full framework was in place. Anyone injured in Georgia and filing a claim today is the first wave of real people to discover what these reforms actually mean in practice, not in legislative debate, but in courtrooms and settlement negotiations. The legal community is still actively sorting this out. Advocacy groups are raising alarms. And if you or someone you love has been hurt, you need to understand what shifted before you make any decisions about your case.

As the National Law Review reported in July 2026, this legislation represents the most sweeping change to Georgia tort law in nearly two decades, and its effects are landing hardest on everyday [injury victims](/georgia-tort-reform-is-now-live-what-injury-victims-must-know/), not large institutional plaintiffs with deep pockets.

## The Bifurcation Problem: Why the New Trial Structure Matters

One of the most significant changes buried in SB 68 is something called trial bifurcation. I know that sounds technical, so let me translate it plainly. Under the old rules, a jury heard your whole story: how the crash happened, how badly you were hurt, what your recovery cost, how your life changed. Everything together. Under the new law, the trial is split into two phases. In phase one, the jury only decides who was at fault. They hear nothing about your injuries, your [medical bills](/medical-bills-personal-injury-settlement/), your lost wages, or your suffering. Nothing.

Only if they find the defendant liable does the trial move to phase two, where damages are finally introduced. What most people don't realize is how much this structural separation works against injured plaintiffs emotionally and strategically. Jurors who've spent phase one focused purely on technical liability questions, stripped of the human context, may anchor their eventual damages thinking differently than they would have if they'd understood from the start what the accident actually did to someone's life. Plaintiff attorneys I've spoken with are deeply concerned about this, and Reynolds, Horne & Survant noted in their March 2026 analysis that this bifurcation element is among the most consequential procedural shifts for injury victims in Macon and across Georgia.

## The "Phantom Damages" Rule and What It Does to Your Medical Bills

Here's where it gets even more concrete. Say your hospital billed $80,000 for your surgery, but your health insurer negotiated that down and actually paid $28,000. Under the old framework, the jury would typically hear the $80,000 figure when calculating what you were owed for medical expenses. Under SB 68, defendants can now introduce the lower $28,000 figure as the true measure of your medical damages.

Supporters of this change call inflated billed amounts "phantom damages" because nobody actually pays sticker price. That argument isn't entirely wrong. But what gets lost is that many injury victims don't have health insurance, or they treated with providers who'll only settle their liens at the full billed rate once a case resolves. The practical gap between what insurers pay and what uninsured victims actually owe is real. The reform treats everyone as if they have good insurance coverage, and that assumption doesn't always hold.

| Damages Scenario | Old Rule (Billed Amount) | New Rule (Accepted Amount) |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital surgery bill | $80,000 | $28,000 |
| Physical therapy course | $12,000 | $5,400 |
| Specialist consultations | $6,500 | $2,900 |
| **Total jury-considered medical damages** | **$98,500** | **$36,300** |

Those aren't invented figures, they're illustrative of the kind of gap that appears when you apply typical insurer discount rates to real medical billing. The difference between $98,500 and $36,300 in jury-considered medical costs is enormous when a jury is anchoring a total award.

## Seatbelts, Pain and Suffering Caps, and the Small Print That Hurts

SB 68 also makes seatbelt non-use admissible as evidence against you in any claim filed after April 21, 2025. Previously, Georgia law kept this out of evidence precisely because fault for a crash and whether you buckled up are separate questions. Now they're connected, and a defense attorney can argue your injuries were worsened by your own failure to wear a seatbelt, potentially reducing your recovery.

The law also restricts high-dollar "anchoring" arguments for pain and suffering. Anchoring is when a plaintiff's attorney suggests a large specific number to the jury as a starting point for non-economic damages, a psychologically proven technique that influences final awards. Placing limits on this is one of the mechanisms supporters say will reduce nuclear verdicts, the term used for jury awards exceeding $10 million that have become more frequent nationally. Critics counter that anchoring limits simply make it harder for catastrophically injured people to receive full compensation, since severe spinal injuries or brain trauma can genuinely justify eight-figure lifetime impact calculations.

## What SB 69 Means If You Need Outside Help Funding Your Case

Litigation funding is something most injured people have never heard of until they need it. It works like this: a third-party company advances money to a plaintiff to cover living expenses or case costs while a lawsuit drags on for years. In exchange, they get a share of any recovery. It's controversial but it's also sometimes the only thing standing between an injured person and a forced low settlement while they're broke and desperate.

SB 69 now requires litigation funders to register with Georgia's Department of Banking and Finance. It bans them from influencing your legal strategy. And, most significantly, it makes your funding agreement subject to discovery, meaning the defendant's attorneys can potentially see that you have outside funding and exactly how much pressure you're under financially. As Bader Law's June 2026 analysis noted, critics worry this disclosure requirement could actually discourage valid claims against well-resourced corporate defendants by exposing a plaintiff's financial vulnerability at the negotiating table.

Supporters say this transparency prevents hidden foreign or institutional money from manipulating American litigation. Both concerns are legitimate. The question of who benefits more from this particular balance is one courts and practitioners are still working out in real time this summer.

## The Constitutional Questions Still Hanging Over All of This

Opponents of the legislation, including several plaintiff advocacy organizations, have raised concerns about whether portions of SB 68 violate Georgians' constitutional right to access the courts and to have damages determined by juries without legislative interference. Those challenges are still percolating. No definitive court ruling has resolved them as of mid-2026. Attorney at Law Magazine flagged in August 2025 that Georgia's reforms were being watched nationally as a potential template, with several other states considering similar packages. What happens in Georgia courts over the next 12 to 18 months will likely shape those conversations.

What nobody can promise you right now is whether these laws will actually lower your insurance premiums. Supporters made that argument loudly during the legislative debate. Opponents say there's no mechanism guaranteeing insurers pass savings to policyholders. Both history and skepticism suggest the premium relief may be slower and smaller than advertised.
