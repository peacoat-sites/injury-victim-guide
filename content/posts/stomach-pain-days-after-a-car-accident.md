Stomach pain that shows up two or three days after a [car accident](/headaches-after-a-car-accident-when-to-worry/) is not just soreness from tensing your muscles. It can be a sign of internal bleeding, organ damage, or a seatbelt injury that your body had no outward reason to flag in the emergency room. If you're reading this because your abdomen started hurting after a crash and you feel like you're overreacting, you're not. Get evaluated by a doctor today, not this weekend.

The delay is the part that catches people off guard. Adrenaline is a genuinely effective painkiller. After a collision, your nervous system floods your body with it, and many internal injuries simply don't produce obvious symptoms for 24 to 72 hours, sometimes longer. That's not unusual biology; that's just how traumatic injuries to the abdomen often behave.

## What Could Actually Be Wrong

The abdomen is packed with organs that don't have a great way to hurt you until they're under real pressure. The spleen is especially vulnerable in side-impact crashes. It can tear partially and bleed slowly, which is why someone walks away from a wreck feeling fine and then collapses two days later. A ruptured spleen is a medical emergency.

Other common culprits include liver lacerations, bruised or perforated bowel (the lap belt pushes directly into the small intestine during a frontal impact), kidney contusions, and internal bleeding around the pancreas. None of these show up on a standard X-ray. If an ER only took plain films and sent you home, that doesn't mean you were cleared.

Abdominal aortic injuries are rarer but worth mentioning because the aorta runs right through the abdomen. High-speed crashes can cause dissections or tears that produce diffuse abdominal pain, back pain, or pain radiating to the legs. This is a life-threatening emergency.

Muscle strain and bruising from the seatbelt itself are far more common and much less dangerous. The honest answer is that you can't tell the difference by feel alone.

## Seatbelt Syndrome

Seatbelts save lives. They also cause a specific, well-documented pattern of injury called seatbelt syndrome, which the medical literature has recognized for decades. The classic presentation is a horizontal bruise across the lower abdomen (called a seatbelt sign), but the bruise doesn't always appear immediately, and not everyone gets visible bruising at all.

The injury pattern typically involves the small bowel, the mesentery (the tissue that anchors your intestines), the lumbar spine at L1-L2, and sometimes the bladder. Children are more vulnerable because their lap belts often sit above the hip bones rather than across them. Adults in improperly positioned seats have similar exposure.

If your pain is concentrated low in the abdomen and you were restrained by a lap-shoulder belt, mention seatbelt syndrome specifically to your doctor. It's not something to drop as a casual observation; it changes what imaging they should order.

## The Diagnostic Gap: What Gets Missed and Why

Emergency rooms are set up to find immediately life-threatening injuries. If your vitals are stable and you're not in acute distress, you're likely to get an X-ray and a brief physical exam and be sent home with instructions to follow up. That's a reasonable triage protocol. It's also why delayed abdominal injuries slip through.

CT scans of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast are the standard imaging for identifying solid organ [injuries after](/how-to-document-injury-for-insurance-claim/) trauma. If you weren't given one in the ER and you're now having symptoms, ask your doctor for one explicitly. Don't accept "it's probably muscle strain" as a final answer until imaging has been done.

A common misconception is that if something serious was wrong, you'd know it right away. That's not true for the abdomen. The peritoneum (the lining of the abdominal cavity) has a delayed inflammatory response, which is part of why pain intensifies over hours or days rather than immediately.

Here's a rough guide to how different abdominal injuries tend to present over time:

| Injury Type | Typical Symptom Onset | Key Warning Signs | Imaging Needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Splenic laceration | 12-72 hours post-impact | Left upper abdomen pain, shoulder tip pain, dizziness | CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast |
| Liver laceration | 12-48 hours | Right upper abdomen or right shoulder pain, nausea | CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast |
| Bowel/mesenteric injury | 24-96 hours | Diffuse or cramping pain, fever, rigidity | CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast |
| Kidney contusion | 24-48 hours | Flank or back pain, blood in urine | CT urogram or CT abdomen |
| Internal bleeding (general) | Variable | Lightheadedness, rapid heart rate, worsening pain | CT or emergency surgery consult |
| Seatbelt syndrome (bowel) | 24-72 hours | Lower abdominal pain, visible bruise, back pain | CT abdomen/pelvis; look for Chance fracture |
| Muscle strain | Within 24 hours, peaks day 2-3 | Localized tenderness, worse with movement | Clinical exam; imaging to rule out above |

If you have lightheadedness, a rapid heart rate, or pain that's spreading rather than staying in one spot, call 911. Those are signs of active internal bleeding.

## Documenting This for an Insurance Claim

Insurance companies have a standard strategy for delayed symptoms: minimize them. An adjuster may note that you didn't report abdominal pain at the scene, didn't go to the ER that day, and therefore suggest the pain is unrelated to the crash. This is a legitimate challenge, and the way you counter it is with documentation.

Start a written log the day symptoms appear. Include the date, time, specific location of pain, severity on a 1-10 scale, and what makes it better or worse. Keep every medical bill, every referral, every diagnosis. If you're prescribed imaging or follow-up and you skip it, that creates a gap in your records that works against you.

The Insurance Information Institute has published guidance on how claims are evaluated, and one consistent theme is that gaps in medical treatment are interpreted as gaps in injury. Don't give the insurer that opening.

Nolo's personal injury resources explain the concept of "causation" in injury claims: you have to show both that you were injured and that the accident caused the injury. A clean paper trail connecting the crash date to your first symptom report to a physician's diagnosis is the backbone of that argument.

Depending on your state, you may have a relatively short window to file a personal injury claim, often two to three years from the date of the accident, but this varies. If your injuries turn out to be serious, talking to a personal injury attorney early is worth the time, even just for a free consultation. Most work on contingency, meaning you pay nothing unless you recover something.

One tool that can genuinely help: a medical records organizer or injury journal kept alongside your medical care. Something like a structured health journal (available on Amazon; this site may earn a commission) gives you a consistent format for daily entries, which looks more credible than a scrambled notebook when it's reviewed months later.

## When to Go Back to the ER

Go immediately if you have any of these:

- Rigid or board-like abdomen (can't press in at all)
- Pain that is spreading to your shoulder tip, especially the left
- Blood in your urine or stool
- Vomiting that won't stop
- Dizziness or fainting
- Fever above 101°F with abdominal pain
- Pain that was improving and suddenly gets much worse

That last one matters. A temporary improvement can happen with some bowel injuries as inflammation settles briefly before worsening. Don't interpret a few hours of feeling better as resolution.
