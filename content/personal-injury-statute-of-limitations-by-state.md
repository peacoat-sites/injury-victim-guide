The single most important deadline after an injury is one most people have never heard of: the statute of limitations. It's the legal time limit to file a lawsuit, and it is unforgiving. Miss it, even by a day, and the court will almost certainly throw out your case no matter how strong it was. This is why the clock matters more than almost anything else in the early days after an accident.

The deadline depends on your state, and the range is wider than people expect, from as little as one year to as long as six.

## Why the date you were injured matters so much

In most cases the clock starts on the date of the injury. From that day, you have the window shown above to either settle your claim or formally file a lawsuit. Settlement negotiations do not pause the clock. Insurance companies know this, and a common tactic is to keep talking cooperatively until your deadline quietly passes, at which point your leverage disappears entirely.

## The exceptions that can change your deadline

The table shows the standard rule, but several situations shift it:

- **The discovery rule.** If you could not reasonably have known you were harmed right away (some medical or toxic-exposure cases), the clock may start when you discovered the injury instead.
- **Minors.** For someone injured as a child, the deadline is often paused until they turn 18.
- **Claims against a government entity.** Suing a city, county, or state agency frequently comes with a much shorter notice deadline, sometimes just 60 to 180 days, that runs separately from the limit above.
- **Wrongful death and medical malpractice** often have their own distinct deadlines that differ from the general personal-injury limit.

## What to do with this

Treat the number for your state as the outer edge, not a target. Evidence fades, witnesses move, and memories blur, so the practical deadline for building a strong case is much sooner. If you think you may have a claim, the safest move is to talk to a licensed attorney in your state as early as possible, ideally within weeks, not months.

*This page is general information, not legal advice, and laws change. Exceptions can shorten or extend these deadlines. Only a licensed attorney in your state can tell you the deadline that applies to your specific situation.*
